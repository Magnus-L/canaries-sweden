### The score, in the four places the obvious construction goes wrong

**1. The floor.** Script 65's `occupation_exposure` drops the uncoded incumbents and only then
applies the floor, so its floor counts **coded** incumbents; and its weight is a single November
head count while script 47j's is person-months summed over 2019. A floor of five therefore means
five coded persons in one month on one route and five person-months on the other, about an order
of magnitude stricter before any coverage question arises. That, and not missing codes, is most
of why **script 70's route ladder falls from 172,396 employers to 60,704**, and it is why that
ladder is NOT the comparison this lane makes. Here the floor is on the firm's **incumbents**, on
incumbent person-months in 2019, summed from 47L's monthly counts, which the script pulls through
47L's own query if `L_counts_2019` is not on the share. If those counts cannot be had the
November head count is used, the summary says so in those words, and the sensitivity at floors
of 1, 3 and 5 is reported either way.

**2. The cascade, and what actually constrains it.** Each incumbent PERSON takes the occupation
recorded for him in 2019, failing that 2018, then 2017, 2016 and 2015. **The years are not the
constraint**: the register dictionary
(`lab-infrastructure/oru-micro-ai/documentation/mona-dictionary/lisa-individual-tables.md`, the
layer that answers this, since the verified-schema catalogue covers neither LISA nor AGI nor RTB
and says so) puts `Individ_YYYY` at 1990 to 2023, so every year the cascade wants exists. **The
column is.** `Ssyk4_2012_J16`, which 47L reads and which includes imputed occupations for the
self-employed, exists **from 2016**; `Ssyk4_2012` is SSYK 2012 at three and four digits **from
2014** and SSYK96 before, a different classification whose codes would merge against DAIOE's keys
and produce silent nonsense rather than a failed merge. So 2019 to 2016 read the J16 column, 2015
reads `Ssyk4_2012`, the export says which column each year used, and a guard refuses to start if
any cascade year falls below 2015 or resolves to a column the dictionary does not list for it.
**Backward only for the reported score** — a code recorded after 2019 would break the paper's
claim that no occupation code recorded after the freeze year enters anything. The forward variant
(2020, 2021) is a separate arm and never the score. The `INFORMATION_SCHEMA` probe is kept as a
**check**: a backward year the delivery does not hold stops the run rather than shortening the
score quietly; a missing forward year is dropped from that arm alone and said so.

**3. The level, and why the uniform one is primary.** Coverage gets worse going back, and the
dictionary says why: *"From 2019 all occupations have at least a 3-digit code, 4-digit from
2023."* DAIOE is keyed on four digits, so an earlier fallback is more likely to be three-digit
and to fail the merge. The score therefore takes **three digits for every incumbent**, from a book
built once as the **2019 national employment-weighted mean of the four-digit DAIOE scores within
each three-digit group**, read from the register's OWN `Ssyk3_2012_J16` column and never by
truncating the four-digit field, which would inherit that field's missingness. The weights are
national and the book is fixed: a firm-specific book would embed the firm's own occupational
composition and stop being an external measure. **The workers lacking a four-digit code are not a
random subset**, so a mixed four-then-three rule would give a sharp score to firms whose coding is
complete and a score smoothed toward group means, and compressed away from the extremes, to firms
whose coding is not; quartile assignment would then depend partly on coding completeness, which
is a bias channel into the treatment variable itself and not merely noise in it. Under the
uniform rule the smoothing is common to every firm and the ranking survives it. **Do not reverse
this: the mixed arm looks more precise and is not.** Three arms are fitted and printed side by
side, the primary named in every export:

| arm | rule | status |
|---|---|---|
| `uniform3` | every incumbent from the three-digit book | **PRIMARY, and what the read rules read** |
| `mixed43` | four digits where usable, the book otherwise | robustness |
| `four_only` | four digits only; the rest leave the mix | robustness |

**4. What the coarsening costs, measured rather than assumed.** The employment-weighted variance
of the four-digit score is decomposed into between and within three-digit groups and both shares
are printed prominently in the summary, beside the mean absolute distance of a four-digit score
from its group's book value, which is the error the coarsening makes on the workers who did not
need it. A verified unweighted benchmark is printed beside them: on the released DAIOE panel
(`daioe-pipeline/data/out/daioe_panel_ssyk2012.dta`, 2023, `exp_change_genai` ranked to a
percentile) the split is **91.7 per cent between and 8.3 per cent within**, with mean distance
**5.5** percentile points, median 3.5 and ninetieth percentile 13.4. Recomputed here on 22
September and reproduced exactly; the counts behind it are 423 occupations in 145 groups of which
30 hold a single occupation, the file holding 429 rows at 2023 of which six carry no genai score,
and 423 is the basis that matters because it is exactly the key set of `daioe_quartiles.dta`,
which is what MONA scores from. **If the weighted within-share comes back above 20 per cent the
summary says so in block capitals**, because at that point the choice of `uniform3` as the primary
arm should be revisited rather than assumed. The count of three-digit groups holding a single
four-digit occupation, and the employment share sitting in them, are exported too: for those
workers the arms are identical by construction.

### What each part runs

- **A, the score and what it covers.** No fit. Where the cascade resolves each incumbent, step by
