## ARTICLE

Since late 2022, U.S. stock prices have risen more than 70 percent while job openings have fallen more than 30 percent. The divergence has become one of the most-discussed economic charts of the AI era — a visual case, many argue, that generative AI is replacing workers, driving down labor demand while boosting corporate profits. Our research, using full-population register data from Sweden and 4.6 million job advertisements, finds that this reading of the aggregate picture is wrong. But the underlying concern about AI and labor markets is not. It is just hidden — and hiding in a place where most aggregate statistics cannot reach.

### The aggregate story: timing is everything

The key to separating AI from other explanations is a timing detail that gets overlooked in most commentary. Sweden's central bank, the Riksbank, began raising interest rates in April 2022 — seven months before ChatGPT launched in November 2022. If monetary tightening is the primary driver of declining job postings, the decline should begin with the rate hike. If generative AI is the culprit, the decline should not appear before late 2022.

The data are unambiguous. Using all advertisements published on Platsbanken, Sweden's public employment service vacancy portal, matched to an occupation-level measure of generative-AI exposure, we find that job postings across all AI-exposure categories fell together starting in April 2022, tracking the Riksbank's rate hikes. Our difference-in-differences estimate for the post–rate hike period is −0.127 (p < 0.01); the post-ChatGPT estimate is −0.062 and statistically indistinguishable from zero (p = 0.11). AI-exposed occupations are also uncorrelated with monetary-policy sensitivity — consistent with the two channels being distinct.

The Swedish scary chart is a monetary policy story, not an AI story. Derek Thompson has argued similarly for the United States, where the decline in job openings tracks the Federal Reserve's tightening cycle rather than the ChatGPT launch.

### What is happening inside firms

The aggregate picture, however, conceals something important. Within the same employers, the composition of employment is shifting in a way that aggregate job counts do not capture.

Using monthly employer-declaration data covering the full Swedish working population — linked to occupation codes and age — we find that employment of workers aged 22–25 in highly AI-exposed occupations has fallen 5.5 percent below comparable occupations within the same employers by mid-2025 (95 percent confidence interval: −5.8 to −4.9 percent). This is our conservative event-study estimate. Poisson estimates — which better handle the zero-heavy distribution of employment counts in individual employer-occupation cells — yield a decline of 16 percent across four consistent specifications, closely matching the 16 percent figure reported for the United States by Brynjolfsson, Chandar, and Chen at the Stanford Digital Economy Lab. Workers aged 31 to 49 in the same occupations are essentially unaffected. Workers over 50 show a small positive gain.

The adjustment is happening through hiring, not layoffs. The hiring margin coefficient is −0.0051 (p < 0.001); the separations coefficient is one-quarter as large (−0.0012, p < 0.01). Sweden's employment-protection law — the "last in, first out" principle — makes it difficult to dismiss incumbent workers, so adjustment loads on the entry margin instead. High–AI-exposure employers are not firing their junior staff. They are not replacing them when they leave, and they are not adding new ones.

This hiring-freeze pattern is not unique to Sweden. Hosseini and Lichtinger, analyzing résumé and job-posting data for 62 million workers across 285,000 U.S. firms, find a 7.7 percent junior employment decline in firms that adopted generative AI — also driven by slower hiring rather than layoffs. The mechanism appears consistent across institutional settings.

### The gender dimension

The finding least discussed in the broader debate about AI and labor markets concerns young women.

Women aged 22–25 in highly AI-exposed occupations have experienced an employment decline roughly twice as large as that of their male peers: −0.016 versus −0.007 in our difference-in-differences estimates, a gap that is statistically significant at p < 0.01. About two-fifths of this gap is explained by occupational composition. Women in Sweden are concentrated in administrative, payroll, and customer-service roles that sit near the top of the AI-exposure distribution: payroll administrators are 82 percent female and rank at the 96th percentile of AI exposure; customer service agents and receptionists are 66 percent female.

But occupational sorting explains only two-fifths of the gap. The remaining three-fifths is within-occupation: young women are experiencing larger employment declines than young men doing the same AI-exposed jobs. We cannot directly identify the mechanism from our data, but the result is not simply a story about which occupations women have historically entered.

This employment finding complements recent simulation work by Gardberg, Heyman, Olsson, and Tåg at the Research Institute of Industrial Economics in Stockholm, who show that pre-AI occupational sorting — the historical channeling of women into specific white-collar roles — will amplify post-AI wage inequality. Our data suggest that employment consequences are already materializing before the wage effects fully accumulate. The International Labour Organisation has reported that female-dominated occupations globally face roughly twice the generative-AI exposure risk of male-dominated ones. In Sweden, that elevated risk is now visible in employer-level hiring decisions.

### Why the evidence diverges across countries

Three studies, three countries, three outcomes. Brynjolfsson et al. find a 16 percent Poisson-estimated employment decline for young U.S. workers in AI-exposed occupations. Our Swedish Poisson estimates cluster in the same range. But Kauhanen and Rouvinen at the Finnish Research Institute of the Finnish Economy find essentially no effect in Finland, replicating a comparable research design on Finnish population data.

We test directly whether this Sweden–Finland divergence is methodological. Reweighting our Swedish sample to match Finland's industry-occupation composition, and applying Finland's preferred exposure measure, leaves our estimated decline at approximately 15 percent — nearly unchanged. The divergence is not an artifact of study design.

What remains as candidate explanations is genuinely uncertain: differences in AI adoption intensity, in the vocational-training pathways that channel young Finns into less-exposed trades, in broader labor-market institutions. The cross-country variation is itself a substantive finding. The same underlying technology appears to be producing different distributional outcomes depending on the institutional setting young workers enter.

### The longer-run concern

Job counts alone understate what is at stake. Entry-level positions in AI-exposed occupations are not just jobs. They are the mechanism through which workers accumulate applied skills, professional networks, and the experiential knowledge that constitutes mid-career capability. An employer who stops bringing new cohorts into AI-exposed roles today is not only saving near-term costs. It is also failing to develop the experienced workers it will need a decade from now.

Brynjolfsson and colleagues call young workers the "canaries in the coal mine" — early signals of a broader adjustment. But canaries signal danger, not just present conditions. If the entry-level hiring freeze persists, what follows is not merely a temporary employment dip. It is a generational skill-formation gap that will be visible in career trajectories and productivity long before it registers in headline unemployment figures.

### What to do

Three implications follow.

Monitoring. Aggregate unemployment and job-posting indices will not catch this adjustment early. Employer-level data at monthly frequency, tracking hiring composition by age and occupation, is what reveals it. Countries with employer-declaration registers — Sweden's AGI is one of very few globally — have a surveillance advantage. Countries without them are flying partly blind, and that blind spot is likely to grow as AI adoption accelerates.

Education. If entry-level positions in AI-exposed fields contract, universities and vocational programs face a mandate to restructure how practical training is delivered — not just to teach students how AI tools work, but to provide the experiential learning that employment previously supplied. The task is not AI literacy in the abstract. It is replicating the apprenticeship function that entry-level work has historically served.

Coverage gaps. In Sweden, recent graduates who cannot find work and have not yet qualified for unemployment-insurance benefits are invisible to the social insurance system. Monitoring whether this group is growing disproportionately in AI-exposed fields should be a policy priority — not because the answer is known, but because the question is now urgent enough to require data.
