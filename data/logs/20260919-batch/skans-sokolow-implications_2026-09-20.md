# What our measurement results mean for Skans and Sokolow, and what theirs mean for us

**20 September 2026.** Written while the last MONA jobs run. Based on their
draft as circulated; nothing quoted or reproduced beyond what is needed to
state the design accurately.

## 1. They cite us, and they cite the number that did not survive

Their text reports that outcomes for young workers in AI-exposed occupations
have deteriorated since ChatGPT in several countries including Sweden, cited
to Lodefalk et al. (2026), and says their own results on young graduates
"concur well" with it. They describe our set-up as very similar to
Brynjolfsson et al.

That citation is to the finding the as-of backtest has now undermined. Their
paper is released on 30 September. **They need to know before then**, and not
because their result depends on ours, but because a corroboration that is
itself an artefact is worth nothing to them and they would rather not learn
it afterwards. Erik is a co-author. This is a phone call, not a footnote.

## 2. Our result does NOT straightforwardly impugn their design, and I want
   to be careful about that

It would be easy, and wrong, to read our Monte Carlo as saying that
education-based exposure is broken. What it says is narrower: that a
WORKER-LEVEL education assignment, taken from the education register as a
stock, reports an age gap of -0.31 in a world with no effect and transmits
only 0.18 of any real gap.

Two features of their design sit outside that finding.

**They classify firms and segments, not individual incumbents.** Our own
firm-level education design, 47i, has an artefact of -0.008 at 22-25 and
passes the pre-committed read rule at every age, against -0.360 for the
worker-level version. Aggregating the mismeasured variable over a whole
workforce washes the misclassification out. Their firm-level construction is
the one our evidence supports, not the one it undermines.

**Their graduate analysis is dated by the graduation itself.** Our staleness
problem exists because an education record describes what someone had
finished some years ago. For a recent graduate the record IS the event, and
there is nothing to be stale relative to. That is a structural immunity, not
a lucky one.

They also did something we did not: they validated the exposure measure
externally, against EU-standardised surveys of individual LLM use at work and
against firm AI adoption, and report that educational scores explain more
worker-level LLM use than occupational scores do. We have no such validation
for DAIOE at SSYK4, and every design in our round shares that measure.

Their stated reason for choosing educations over occupations is that
occupations are updated with a delay and cover small firms incompletely. They
saw the problem and designed around it before we measured it.

## 3. What we should take from them

**A graduation-cohort design.** Classify workers by field of education at
graduation, restricted to recent graduates, and follow entry outcomes. The
exposure is contemporaneous by construction, so the register lag cannot touch
it, and the population is exactly the entry margin where our own evidence
puts whatever effect exists. This is the one design idea in their paper that
we do not have and could add without duplicating them: they study graduates
of exposed FIELDS, and the within-employer contrast, which is ours, is not
what they do.

**External validation of the exposure measure.** Every one of our designs
rests on DAIOE at SSYK4 and none of them tests it. If DAIOE mismeasures
genAI exposure, all four fail together and their agreement means nothing.
Validating against an LLM-use survey is a cheap and serious improvement, and
they have shown it can be done in Swedish data.

## 4. The Nordic pattern, which is now worth facing

Their footnote records that the corresponding patterns have not been found in
Finland (Kauhanen and Rouvinen) or Denmark (Humlum and Vestergaard). Set
beside our own result, that is no longer an awkward discrepancy to be
explained away. Three Nordic countries with comparable registers, and the one
that reported a large effect reported it through occupation codes that arrive
two years late. That is a coherent picture rather than a puzzle, and it is
considerably more interesting than a fourth null.

## 5. Consequences for our paper

We should cite them, and cite them as strengthening rather than competing.
Their firm-level education exposure and our firm-level result agree. Their
graduate-hiring finding and our hiring-margin point estimate point the same
way. Our contribution is the within-employer contrast and the measurement
audit; theirs is the segment-level structural-change account with external
validation. These are complements.

What we must not do is cite them as independent corroboration of the original
-0.17 while knowing that they cite us for it. That would be circular, and
somebody would notice.
